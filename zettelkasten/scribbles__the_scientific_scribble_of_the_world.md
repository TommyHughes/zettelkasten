| 9     |          | For example, let's look at the Physicist's "dynamical law". These are the so-called laws of motion: Newtonian, Schrödinger Equation, Least Action.<br><br>But what *is* a law really? Suppose I kick a rock. It tumbles. Why? Why does it move at all? If it does move, why does it move in the specific way that it does?<br><br>What Physics gives me is a "dynamical law"; a principle which characterizes the movements of the rock as it tumbles. The explanation might start with a report about "forces" and end up with a conversation about least action, information axioms, or wave functions.<br><br>But what all these explanations do is characterize the way the rock moves in such detail that they generalize to principles that characterize the trajectory or paths all changes satisfy; they describe the characteristics of the way the world changes and how it does it.<br><br>But my question is why that one and not another. If Physics gives me a dynamical law, I'd ask "why this law" or better "why a law at all"? Even further "What must be true for law-like behavior to even be possible" and then why that?                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |           |
| 10    |          | Even if we postpone our questioning about why a given law holds and not another, and accept the laws that Physics gives us, I might still ask "how should this law be understood?"<br><br>For example, let's go back to my rock. When we inspect the relevant physics, we find no rock laws. There are no laws of physics that describe how rocks behave. Instead we get a relatively compact collection of formulae and principles which we assume the rock, and all things, “obey”.<br><br>There’s an assumed universality and reductionism baked into these laws. They describe how particles or fields or some other physical variables “behave”, but no rock formulas. The rock is an assemblage of these more basic compressed laws that know nothing, in themselves, about rocks. Indeed, it is by these laws that rocks even come to be.<br><br>But how do we know that? The physicist will write down some very tidy formulas that apply broadly, but what is the physical principle that guarantees this is actually what is happening; that this is what things are doing? Why couldn’t it be the case that what the world is actually isn't following any law at all, where each happening is unique, non-homogeneous, and non-universal. That is, there is no electromagnetism, there is no gravity, there are no relations, no cause and effect, there is only the action itself that implies nothing about anything else.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |           |
| 11    |          | Don’t get me wrong I don’t expect the physicist to set up an experiment to verify that. If all the behavior is the same, then these two descriptions are equivalent.”<br><br>And I would applaud such a question. I think it’s a good first step towards realizing the limits of the empirical enterprise.<br><br>A lot of physicists these days seem to have taken up a kind of philosophical activism. For them, there is a need for physics to not *merely* be useful, but “true”. So much so that they urge us to accept that physics is all that there is, that there is no metaphysics. That all there is to reality are physical laws, systems of physical relations. That there is no such thing as “magic” or “soul”, or rather that the soul just is these physical laws. | 13    |          | And I understand and am sympathetic to this idea. But I think it's wrong.<br><br>There is nothing about physics, or empiricism more broadly, that entails truth. It's primary goal is also the limit of its capabilities which is to be useful.<br><br>I think while physicalists have characterized belief in God and the soul as a kind of cope, an "opium for the masses", this applies but in reverse. Physicalists don't want physics and the empirical sciences to be *merely* useful, they want them to be true. This insistence that there is just this physical world of relation and law (REF-10) is what grants them the ability to believe that what they are doing is more than just a useful fiction. Understanding is what is produced by studying nature, each turn of her parts, and compressing those observations into a mathematical theory. This is what many physicalists have dedicated their lives to doing. This is how they have chosen to spend their time on Earth. This is what gives their lives meaning, so it should come as no surprise when they elevate their craft to something more than just toolmaking: "it is the true story of the world we all live in, and you must accept this as well".<br><br>There are ways of encountering the world that aren't logical, coherent, or articulate. The world is larger than any logical system and its under no obligation to make sense to us. Insisting that it does make sense, just so that you can prepare finer predictions of the future and speculation of the past is just a pastime; beavers build dams, spiders spin webs, and some people come up with scientific theories.<br><br>None of this implies that scientists should "give up and go home."
